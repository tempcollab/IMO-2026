## imo-2026-06
- Distinct openings:
  1. **Bounded-prime intersection conjecture (strong direct-arithmetic target).** Let \(P_0=\{p\text{ prime}:p\le a_1\}\). Computation strongly suggests that every two terms have a common prime in \(P_0\). This is substantially stronger and more concrete than merely asserting existence of some finite controller. If true, admissibility is determined by the finite traces \(\operatorname{supp}(a_i)\cap P_0\), hence directly by residues modulo \(\prod_{p\le a_1}p\). The natural contradiction setup is a closest/earliest pair whose \(P_0\)-traces are disjoint. Their common prime factors all exceed \(a_1\), so their numerical separation exceeds \(a_1\), while every sequence gap is at most \(a_1\) (multiples of \(a_1\) are always admissible). A closest bad pair forces each intervening term’s bounded-prime trace to meet both endpoint traces. This “bridge interval” is the plausible arithmetic place to obtain a density or divisibility contradiction. The hard step is converting those forced bridge traces plus bounded gaps into an impossible count; merely observing that an intervening term exists is not enough.
  2. **Finite-state recurrence on blocks of length \(a_1\).** Every interval \((a_n,a_n+a_1]\) contains an admissible multiple of \(a_1\), so the gap word \(d_n=a_{n+1}-a_n\) takes values in \(\{1,\dots,a_1\}\). Track a block by residues modulo \(M=\prod_{p\le a_1}p\) together with which bounded-prime trace types have appeared. This gives a genuinely finite recurrence if constraints carried only by primes \(>a_1\) can be shown redundant after they leave a bounded window. The attractive endpoint would be repetition of a deterministic block state, yielding periodic gaps and therefore \(a_{n+T}=a_n+L\). The hard step is precisely a finite-memory lemma for large primes: a large prime can recur at distant multiples, so bounded gaps alone do not erase it.
  3. **Divisibility-poset / minimal large-prime edge.** Form the graph on terms with an edge labelled by a chosen common prime; seek a minimal-span edge whose every label exceeds \(a_1\). Since a prime \(q>a_1\) cannot divide two terms at distance at most \(a_1\), no consecutive sequence pair can be such an edge. Minimal span then makes every internal vertex connect to each endpoint through bounded-prime edges, imposing two disjoint divisibility requirements on all bridge terms. This is a possible route to a packing bound on bridge terms (multiples of products \(pr\), with \(p,r\le a_1\)) versus the guaranteed maximum gap \(a_1\). Hard steps: labels need not be unique, traces can use many primes, and a naive density union bound is too weak without choosing an extremal endpoint pair carefully.
  4. **Explicit periodicity contradiction.** Assume the gap word never becomes periodic modulo \(M=\prod_{p\le a_1}p\). Two equal finite residue/block states must occur by pigeonhole; any different continuation must be witnessed by an earlier term sharing with one candidate only through a prime \(q>a_1\). Choosing the first disagreement isolates such a \(q\)-witness. Since equal states occur at a displacement divisible by \(M\), bounded primes behave identically, while the exceptional large prime forces a divisibility relation on that displacement or on two candidates. This is far from the upfamily framing, but the unresolved issue is obtaining a genuinely finite state before invoking pigeonhole; recording all active large-prime witnesses would be infinite-state.
- Candidate technique(s): bounded-gap extremal principle; closest bad pair in a divisibility graph; finite-state recurrence modulo a primorial; density/packing of multiples in a bridge interval; first-disagreement periodicity contradiction. The most plausible concrete target is the conjecture that all pairwise gcd intersections are already witnessed by a prime at most \(a_1\).
- Cheap-kill candidates: (i) **uniform gap bound** \(a_{n+1}-a_n\le a_1\), because the next multiple of \(a_1\) is admissible: every term has nontrivial gcd with \(a_1\); (ii) therefore a prime \(q>a_1\) cannot divide consecutive terms; (iii) if two terms have no common prime \(\le a_1\), their difference is a nonzero multiple of some common \(q>a_1\), hence exceeds \(a_1\) and there is at least one intervening term; (iv) each term contains some prime divisor of \(a_1\), although those initial-prime traces need not be pairwise intersecting (e.g. \(a_1=105\)). These facts should be exhausted before heavier density estimates.
- Knowledge-base entries to use: **Divisor analysis**; **Modular arithmetic, CRT**; **Linear recurrences** (finite deterministic states are eventually periodic); **Comparability / divisibility graphs**; **Pigeonhole / extremal principle**; **Invariants & monovariants**; and the general **Reformulate**, **Contradiction**, and **Solve a simpler / special case first** heuristics. No valuation, LTE, Zsigmondy, or Dirichlet input appears naturally relevant.

- Dead ends (do not retry): the one-stage finite-anchor construction (selected witnesses do not prove positive traces remain positive); CRT avoidance without a compatible assignment mechanism; first-disagreement arguments that silently assume finite memory; branch induction with the false residual-family reduction. Also do not conjecture control by \(\operatorname{supp}(a_1)\): it is false as a pairwise-trace claim. For \(a_1=105\), terms 108 and 110 have disjoint traces on \(\{3,5,7\}\), while their common factor 2 is still \(\le a_1\).
- Small-case / intuition notes: **Conjectural evidence only.** Direct generation of the first 1000 terms for every \(2\le a_1\le500\) found no pair of terms whose common prime divisors are all greater than \(a_1\). Equivalently, all tested traces on primes \(\le a_1\) were pairwise intersecting. Testing admissibility against the bounded-prime traces likewise found no discrepancy in broad finite ranges. Typical sequences are immediately periodic: prime/power starts collapse to multiples of the least prime factor; \(a_1=15\) has period shift \((T,L)=(8,30)\). Composite starts such as 105 exhibit many trace types and show why the threshold must include primes not dividing \(a_1\). The evidence points to the explicit controller \(P_0=\{p\le a_1\}\), but no proof of its pairwise-intersection property is presently established.
