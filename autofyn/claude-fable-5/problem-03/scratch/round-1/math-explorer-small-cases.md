- Dead ends (do not retry):
  - **Naive equal-spacing conjecture** `c(n) = (n+1)/(2n+1)` (i.e. LB places marks to eventually make all 2n+1 final pieces equal to 1/(2n+1)): this WORKS for n=1 (giving 2/3) but FAILS for n=2. Concretely, if LB marks at 1/5, 3/5 (pieces 0.2, 0.4, 0.4), XY can respond with a single point splitting the 0.2 piece into two 0.1's, producing pieces {0.4, 0.4, 0.1, 0.1} — a perfectly paired multiset — forcing an exact 50/50 split (LB only gets 0.5, far below the conjectured 0.6). Verified by exact construction and independently by numerical optimization (`xy_best_response` search, see below). **Any approach that has LB aim for "all pieces eventually equal" is refuted for n≥2.**
  - Related dead end: any LB strategy producing two or more *equal* (or very close) pieces among its own n+1 initial pieces is vulnerable to this "pairing" counter — XY only needs enough budget to split the odd-piece(s)-out to match, and if LB's pieces have an exploitable rational relationship, XY may approximate the pairing well enough to still force close to 50/50. Verified numerically: LB configurations with two equal or near-equal "large" pieces (e.g. (0.6,0.2,0.2), (0.5,0.25,0.25), (1/3,1/3,1/3), and even very skewed (0.98,0.01,0.01)) all cap out at exactly XY-total = 0.5 in my optimizer, regardless of how large the top piece is — the top piece just gets bisected by XY to make a matching pair.

- Small-case / intuition notes (labeled as conjectures/computed facts):
  - **n=1 solved by hand, high confidence** (elementary case analysis, not just numerics): LB marks the single point at x=1/3, creating pieces {1/3, 2/3}. For any x ≤ 1/2, I derived analytically (full case split on where XY's one extra point falls) that XY's best response value is max(x, (1-x)/2), giving LB total = min(1-x, (1+x)/2); this is uniquely maximized at x=1/3, giving **c(1) = 2/3**. Direct check: XY's best reply to x=1/3 is to split the 2/3 piece into two 1/3's, giving three equal pieces of 1/3 each; LB (first, third picks) gets 2/3, XY (second pick) gets 1/3. This is a clean, essentially rigorous result (modulo formalizing "greedy claiming is optimal," which is separately verified below) and should be treated as a solid anchor, not just a conjecture.
  - **Greedy-claiming lemma verified computationally**: for the claiming subgame (fixed fixed multiset of piece lengths, players alternately pick any remaining piece, additive payoff = sum of picks), the optimal play by both sides is "always take the current largest remaining piece," verified against exact minimax (dynamic-programming over subsets) on 2000 random multisets of size 1–7: zero mismatches. This should be provable directly (exchange argument) and is a prerequisite lemma for everything else.
  - **n=2 conjecture, strong numerical support**: LB's optimal marks produce initial pieces in ratio 4:2:1, i.e. LB marks at 4/7 and 6/7 (pieces {4/7, 2/7, 1/7}). With very high-precision numerical optimization (150 random restarts per XY sub-strategy, exhaustively enumerating how XY distributes its ≤2 extra points among the 3 existing pieces, then L-BFGS-B on the continuous split fractions), XY's best response against {4/7,2/7,1/7} was found to be **exactly 3/7** (matching to 15 significant digits, i.e. essentially certainly exact, not just close), giving **LB total = 4/7 ≈ 0.5714**. I also explored many nearby non-geometric configurations; with insufficient optimizer restarts these initially looked like they beat 4/7 (up to ~0.578), but re-running with much higher restart counts on those same configurations showed XY actually forces LB down to *below* 4/7 there — i.e. those apparent improvements were optimizer noise (the inner XY-optimizer failing to find its true best response at generic points), not genuine. This gives me reasonably high confidence that **c(2) = 4/7 exactly**, though I could not fully rule out an even better LB configuration by hand (only by extensive but imperfect numerical search).
  - **Closed-form conjecture**: `c(n) = 2^n / (2^{n+1} - 1)`. Matches c(1) = 2/3 exactly (analytically) and c(2) = 4/7 (numerically, to high confidence). Equivalent forms: `c(n) = 1/2 + 1/(2(2^{n+1}-1))`, and `1 - c(n) = (2^n - 1)/(2^{n+1}-1)`. As n→∞, c(n) → 1/2 from above (monotonically decreasing in n) — this is intuitively sensible: with more marks available to both sides the game becomes more balanced in relative-share terms (though LB's absolute number of pieces claimed, n+1 out of 2n+1, still gives a strict majority of *pieces*, just not much extra *length* per piece as n grows). This candidate should be checked against the alternative forms `(n+1)/(2n+1)` and `1/2+1/(4n+2)` (these are algebraically identical to each other and are REFUTED by the n=2 computation: they predict c(2)=3/5=0.6, which the numerics clearly reject in favor of 4/7≈0.571).
  - **Conjectured optimal construction for general n**: LB marks points splitting [0,1] into n+1 pieces of lengths `2^n/(2^{n+1}-1), 2^{n-1}/(2^{n+1}-1), ..., 2/(2^{n+1}-1), 1/(2^{n+1}-1)` (a geometric sequence with ratio 1/2, largest piece first). This is UNVERIFIED beyond n=1,2 but is the natural inductive guess given the pattern, and is the most promising concrete construction for the outliner to try to prove optimal (both the lower bound — LB can guarantee ≥ c(n) with this construction against every XY response — and the matching upper bound — XY has a response capping LB at exactly c(n) against ANY LB configuration).
  - **Adversary mechanism identified (important for the upper-bound proof)**: two competing XY counter-strategies emerged in my numerics against a given LB partition q1 ≥ q2 ≥ ... ≥ q_{n+1} (summing to 1): (A) if q1 ≥ 1 − q1 (i.e. q1 ≥ 1/2), XY can spend n−1 cuts splitting q1 into exact copies of q2,...,q_{n+1} plus a leftover remainder r = 2q1 − 1, giving LB total exactly q1 (this was verified exactly by hand for n=2: pieces become {q2,q2,q3,q3,r}, and the odd-rank sum always telescopes to exactly q1 regardless of where r ranks). (B)/(C) XY can instead just bisect the current largest piece (using only 1 cut) to create a new top pair, which becomes attractive once q1 is large enough relative to q2 that halving it still leaves a dominant pair — this was the mechanism that capped LB at exactly 0.5 in all the "two large near-equal pieces" dead-end cases above. The optimal LB configuration is exactly the point where neither (A) nor (B) can improve on the other for XY — this "crossover/tie" condition is likely the right lever for a clean general-n proof.
