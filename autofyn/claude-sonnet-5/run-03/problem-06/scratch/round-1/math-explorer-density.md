- Distinct openings (density/gap-growth lens):
  1. **Finite-state pigeonhole via bounded auxiliary quantity** (the strongest opening found). Mirror the two-solution structure of the closely analogous ISL/IMO problem `aimo-0678` (see below): find some auxiliary numeric quantity attached to the process that is *provably bounded* (there, `a_n` itself was bounded; here the natural analog is "the number of *still-unresolved* prior-term gcd obligations" or "the finite set of primes that must be checked for the next candidate"), then reduce the *unbounded* part of the state (here, `a_n` itself, or the residue pattern of recently-used primes) modulo the lcm of the bounded part's possible values. This turns the whole update rule into a map on a **finite state space**, and finite deterministic maps are eventually periodic by pigeonhole (rho-shape: pre-period + cycle) — this is exactly the shape of the required conclusion `a_{n+T} = a_n + L`. This is a genuinely different top-level target from "track prime supports combinatorially forever": here the target is "exhibit a finite state space and a deterministic transition on it," and periodicity is *automatic* once that's established, no separate periodicity argument needed.
  2. **Sieve/inclusion-exclusion gap bound (Jacobsthal-style).** If one can show that only a *fixed finite* set of primes S ever becomes "permanently active" (i.e. eventually every sufficiently large term is divisible by at least one prime of S), then by inclusion–exclusion the density of integers divisible by at least one prime of S is `1 - φ(N)/N` where `N = lcm(S)` (or `= prod(S)` if S is a prime set), which is bounded away from 0; this gives an explicit, N-dependent upper bound on all gaps `a_{n+1}-a_n` (a Jacobsthal-function-type bound). This proves the gaps are *bounded* — useful supporting fact, but on its own it does NOT prove periodicity, only boundedness. It's a lemma-strength tool, not a route to the full theorem by itself.
  3. **Complement/counting framing.** Numbers coprime to the full history look sparse once enough primes accumulate; but computationally (see below) the "active prime set" needed to explain the sequence does NOT grow without bound — it appears to stabilize quickly (e.g. {2,3,5,7} for a_1=105). A counting argument bounding *how many distinct primes* can remain "permanently required" (i.e. show the total prime demand is finite) could be attempted via a potential/monovariant on the total "backlog," but I could not find a clean density argument for this in the time available — this looks more amenable to the prime-bookkeeping lens than to pure density, and should probably be handed to that route; flagging it here as a genuine shared gap.

  - If `a_1` is a single odd prime power or a number with one odd prime factor `p` alone but the primes 2,3 aren't yet present (e.g. `a_1=3,5,9,25`): sequence becomes `a_1, a_1+a_1, a_1+2a_1, ...` i.e. all multiples of the smallest prime factor `p` of `a_1` — trivial period `T=1, L=p`.

  - `a_1=105=3·5·7` (chosen specifically to NOT include 2, forcing a genuinely 4-prime {2,3,5,7} eventual structure since 2 gets pulled in at step 2): gaps become periodic from the very start (no transient at all, in this case: pigeonhole "cycle" begins immediately) with **period `T=58`, shift `L=210=lcm(2,3,5,7)`**, confirmed over 6000+ terms and cross-checked as `a_{n+58}=a_n+210` exactly for the sampled range. Max observed gap is 6, min is 2 (consistent with a Jacobsthal-type bound for N=210, whose true Jacobsthal function value is g(210)=10, i.e. observed gaps are comfortably inside that bound — so the "gap boundedness" sub-claim looks safely provable via inclusion-exclusion/sieve, but note the *exact* gap sequence is finer than the coprimality-sieve density alone: e.g. `T=58` is emphatically NOT `φ(210)=48` nor `210-φ(210)=162` — no simple closed-form guess worked, so the exact `(T,L)` values must come from tracking the actual deterministic finite-state map, not from a density formula. This confirms opening 1 (finite-state pigeonhole) is the right target: it explains *why* periodicity holds without needing to *guess* T, L in closed form — the state-space argument only needs "finite ⇒ eventually periodic," and T, L fall out as whatever the actual cycle length/shift are, not as objects requiring a separate formula.
  - Surprising fact worth flagging to the outliner: within one period (e.g. the a_1=105 run), the "permanent obligation" toward the very first term a_1=105 (share a factor with {3,5,7} forever) recurs exactly once per period as an *odd* multiple of 105 (e.g. term 315 = 3·105 appears at the position marking the period boundary) — i.e. periodicity doesn't force *every* term to be divisible by 2; only a density-plurality of them are. This shows the eventual behavior is NOT simply "all terms divisible by a single fixed prime" but a genuinely periodic *pattern* of prime usage across a full period — supports treating the whole cycle (not a single steady prime set) as the right invariant object.
