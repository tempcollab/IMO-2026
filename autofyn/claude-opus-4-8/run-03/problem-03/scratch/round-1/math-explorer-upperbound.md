## imo-2026-03 (lens: Xiang Yu's counter-strategy / upper bound c(n) ≤ ...)

### Verified reduction (foundational lemma, must still be proved rigorously)
On a fixed finite multiset of pieces, the alternating "claim any unclaimed piece, each maximizing own total" game (Liu Bang first) is won by the greedy rule: sort pieces descending b_1≥b_2≥...≥b_m; Liu Bang's optimal total = b_1+b_3+b_5+... (odd ranks), Xiang Yu gets the even ranks. This is a standard exchange-argument fact (no positional adjacency constraint, unlike a line/array game — free choice of *any* piece makes "always take the current largest" optimal for both sides simultaneously). Treat this as an assumed/must-cite lemma, not yet independently re-derived here — flag for the outliner to include a short proof (swap argument: if a player ever takes a non-largest available piece, swapping that pick with the largest weakly helps them and weakly hurts no future move).

Given this, if Liu Bang uses all n marks (n+1 pieces) and Xiang Yu uses all n marks (adds n more cuts, total ≤ 2n+1 final pieces, generically exactly 2n+1), Liu's total = sum of odd ranks among 2n+1 sorted pieces (n+1 terms), Xiang's = sum of even ranks (n terms).

### Key reframing (useful for both bound directions)
Write final sorted pieces b_1≥...≥b_{2n+1} (sum 1). Then
Liu total − Xiang total = (b_1−b_2)+(b_3−b_4)+...+(b_{2n−1}−b_{2n}) + b_{2n+1} =: D ≥ 0.
Since Liu+Xiang = 1: **Liu total = 1/2 + D/2.** So Xiang Yu's entire job is to choose his ≤n cuts to make D — the sum of the n consecutive "odd-minus-even" rank gaps plus the final leftover smallest piece — as small as possible, regardless of Liu's a_1≥...≥a_{n+1}. Equivalently Xiang wants to pair up pieces into (rank 2k−1, rank 2k) twins that are as close in value as possible, with only a tiny sliver left unpaired at the bottom.

### Numerical findings (conjecture, verified by direct nonlinear optimization, not proof)
- n=1: solved essentially by hand. Liu's optimal single cut is at x=2/3 (pieces 2/3, 1/3). Xiang Yu's best response: split the 2/3 piece into two 1/3's, giving three equal pieces 1/3,1/3,1/3 — D=0 exactly attained (not just approached), Liu total = 2/3. For x<2/3 Xiang instead attacks the *small* piece with a near-degenerate split to approach (not attain) an even smaller Liu total; for x>2/3 Xiang bisects the large piece; the crossover x=2/3 is where both options coincide and is Liu's true optimum. So **c(1) = 2/3**.
- n=2: ran a nested numerical optimization (Liu's a≥b≥c grid search outer, Xiang's best-response over all cut-count splits (k1,k2,k3), k1+k2+k3≤2, with Nelder–Mead inner optimization over split points, many random restarts to avoid false optima). Found the maximum robustly at **a=4/7, b=2/7, c=1/7** (verified stable under refinement with up to 100 restarts; 15 nearby grid points, including ones that looked competitive under low-effort search, all dropped below 4/7 once the inner optimizer was run to convergence — earlier "beats 4/7" hits were optimizer artifacts from too few restarts). Value: **c(2) = 4/7 ≈ 0.5714**.
  - Sanity check of a wrong-looking config: Liu picks (0.4,0.4,0.2) (i.e. equal big pieces) — Xiang's best response splits the *small* piece 0.2 into two 0.1's (using only 1 of his 2 allowed cuts!): final {0.4,0.4,0.1,0.1}, D=0 exactly, Liu total = 0.5. This is much worse for Liu than 4/7, confirming "equal/symmetric" Liu configurations are bad, and that Xiang Yu sometimes needs only *some* of his budget.
  - At the tight config (4/7,2/7,1/7): Xiang splits the 4/7 piece into two 2/7's (1 cut), giving {2/7,2/7,2/7,1/7}; ranks 1,3 for Liu = 2/7+2/7=4/7 exactly. A second cut (e.g. splitting a 2/7 into two 1/7's, or splitting the 1/7) never improves on 4/7 — multiple Xiang responses tie at exactly 4/7, confirming tightness.
- **Conjectured closed form: c(n) = 2^n / (2^{n+1} − 1)**, matching n=1 (2/3) and n=2 (4/7 — numerically confirmed to ~1e-9 precision, robust to reoptimization).
- Extremal Liu configuration conjecture: the n+1 pieces are a **dyadic geometric sequence** 2^n, 2^{n-1}, ..., 2, 1 (all divided by 2^{n+1}−1). Xiang Yu's matching response on this config: repeatedly split the current top piece into two copies of the next piece down (2^k → two 2^{k-1}'s), recursively — this is the strategy that achieves D = 1/(2^{n+1}−1) exactly on the extremal input.

### Candidate technique(s) for the upper bound (my lens)
- The reframing "Liu total = 1/2 + D/2" turns the upper bound into: **for every a_1≥...≥a_{n+1} (sum 1), Xiang Yu has a way, using ≤n cuts, to force D ≤ 1/(2^{n+1}−1)**, where D is the alternating-gap-plus-tail of the *final* 2n+1 (or fewer) sorted pieces.
- The correct Xiang strategy is **not** a single fixed rule (a hard-found gap): "always bisect the current largest piece" fails on some configs (e.g. Liu picks x=0.6 for n=1 — bisecting the large piece gives D=0.4 not the optimal ~0.2 achievable by attacking the smaller piece instead). Likewise "always split the smallest to pair the top two" fails when the top pieces are far apart (need to split the top piece itself, e.g. the dyadic extremal case). The real strategy is **adaptive/recursive matching**: greedily pair off the two largest still-unprocessed pieces by splitting whichever is needed to make them (nearly) equal, recursing on the remainder — essentially a Huffman-style greedy merge. This adaptive pairing procedure, and an inductive proof that it forces D ≤ 1/(2^{n+1}−1) with equality only at the dyadic configuration, is the central missing piece (the hard step) for a rigorous upper-bound proof. A natural attack: strong induction on n, peeling off the top piece a_1 and comparing a_1 to a_2 (or to the sum of the rest) — reminiscent of the aimo-0117 "biggest played value dominates the sum of the rest" dyadic-ladder argument (see below).
- Also worth verifying as a lemma (not yet proved here): Liu Bang should always use all n of his marks (using fewer is weakly dominated) — likely provable by a "split off a negligible sliver" continuity argument, but flag it as unverified.

### Cheap-kill candidates
- **Symmetric/equal-piece Liu configurations are provably suboptimal**: any config with two equal (or near-equal) largest pieces lets Xiang Yu neutralize them almost for free by bisecting a smaller piece to match — this immediately kills any "equal thirds," "equal halves," or generally non-dyadic symmetric guesses as candidates for Liu's optimal strategy. Useful to quickly rule out wrong constructions the outliner might propose.
- A parity/counting cheap-kill: total final pieces is 2n+1 (odd) when both sides use all cuts, so Liu, going first, always nets exactly one more piece (by count) than Xiang — this doesn't by itself bound the *value*, but it explains why Liu total > Xiang total always (D≥0, strict unless perfectly paired plus b_{2n+1}=0), and constrains any claimed formula to give c(n) > 1/2 for all n (2^n/(2^{n+1}-1) > 1/2 for all n, consistent check).

### Analogous past problems (cruxes)
- **aimo-0117** (Dutch olympiad, "Jesse en Tjeerd" stone/box game) — genuinely analogous in spirit: an adversarial alternating game where the winning construction is a **two-sided geometric (dyadic, powers-of-2) sequence of values such that the largest value strictly exceeds the sum of all smaller ones** (`2^j > 2^{j-1}+...+2^{-i}`), and the strategy maintains an invariant that "the currently-largest played value sits in the favored box," extending the dyadic ladder in response to the opponent's disruption. This is a strong structural match for our numerically-discovered extremal configuration (2^n,...,2,1)/(2^{n+1}−1) and suggests the general upper-bound proof should also hinge on a dyadic-domination potential/invariant (largest piece dominating the rest), not a naive equal-split heuristic. Crux move to adapt: "assign/construct values as a two-sided geometric sequence so the top value dominates the sum of the rest," and "maintain an invariant about which box holds the currently-largest value, extend the ladder on each disruption." Worth reading in full for the outliner (`past_problems_database.json`, id `aimo-0117`).

### Dead ends (do not retry)
- "Xiang Yu always bisects the current largest piece" as a universal strategy — **fails**: counterexample Liu picks x=0.6 for n=1 (pieces 0.6,0.4); bisecting the large piece gives final {0.3,0.3,0.4}, D=0.4 hence Liu total=0.7, whereas Xiang can actually force Liu down toward 0.6 by instead cutting the small piece near-degenerately. Confirmed by hand calculation and numerically.
- "Liu Bang's optimal configuration is equal pieces" (1/(n+1) each) — **fails**: numerically for n=2, equal thirds (1/3,1/3,1/3) let Xiang force Liu down to only 0.5 (verified: `xiang_best_overall(1/3,1/3,1/3) ≈ 0.5`), far below the true optimum 4/7≈0.571. Equal or near-equal big pieces are exactly what Xiang Yu neutralizes most easily (split a smaller piece to pair with the tied big ones, D→0).
